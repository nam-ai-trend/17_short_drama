# Story guidance for the Antigravity bridge

Generate three genuinely different synopsis options. Give each a genre engine, a concrete first-frame hook, the audience's question, one early foreshadowing object or behavior, a causally earned reveal, and an emotional payoff. Keep the existing `title`, `logline`, `hook`, `bridge`, `aha`, and `payoff` proposal fields so the dashboard can display them. Also return `protagonist_choice` (the costly or risky choice the lead makes on screen), `reveal_evidence` (the earlier observable clue or credible proof that makes the reversal believable), and `payoff_action` (what someone physically does differently after the reveal). These are short concrete descriptions, not theme labels.

Return exactly three options. For each option also return `genre_engine` and `hook_type` (stable snake_case labels). Give the options three different genre engines and three different hook types, unless the user's explicit brief makes that impossible. Changing only names, jobs, locations, or props does not make an option structurally different. The three options should offer different audience emotions: for example cathartic reversal, warm repayment, and poignant reconciliation. Keep the same reference premise only as broad inspiration; change the dramatic mechanism and distinctive scenes. A hidden rich person, an abusive gatekeeper, or a sudden benefactor must not appear in every option.

Test each hook against the first frame: can a viewer see the conflict, odd choice, or missing object without a title card or backstory? Specify the first physical action and, if useful, one short line. Use one of these hook families across the three options: `confrontation` (conflict already underway), `strange_action` (a person deliberately does something puzzling), `missing_object` (an absence or swap changes a relationship), `unfair_choice` (someone must choose between two losses), `visual_contrast` (status or expectation visibly conflicts with behavior), `interrupted_payoff` (an expected outcome is visibly blocked). Do not force insulting dialogue into a gentle or mystery-led story.

Genre engines to choose from: earned kindness or repayment; hidden identity; self-inflicted consequence; family reconciliation; a misunderstanding that heals; a concise comic reversal; or low-dialogue visual action. Choose according to the user's reference and notes. Do not make all three options the same identity reveal. Do not force humiliation into a warm story.

Adapted genre patterns from the reference library:
- `self_inflicted_consequence`: the aggressor's own choice causes a visible setback; the protagonist does not need to punish them.
- `hidden_identity`: an underestimated person's role is revealed through an early clue and another character's reaction.
- `earned_kindness`: a small kindness returns in a specific, earned form; the final image shows changed circumstances.
- `family_reconciliation`: a loved one's hidden effort is discovered through an object or habit and leads to a changed choice.
- `misunderstanding`: evidence first supports a painful interpretation, then a fairer one; both characters act on the truth.
- `fraud_reversal`: a deceptive approach is exposed by a small contradiction and a clear consequence, without teaching a real fraud method.
- `community_solution`: a local conflict is resolved by practical knowledge, giving both sides a visible reason to soften.
- `late_life_romance`: resistance or embarrassment yields to a sincere gesture and an earned response.
- `animal_proxy`: low-dialogue visual action lets an animal's behavior reveal or resolve a conflict without harm.
- `narrated_reveal`: a stable narrator compresses setup, leaving only the decisive spoken lines for characters.
- `next_day_reversal`: an encounter in a neutral place is reinterpreted in the protagonist's own domain after a motivated transition.
- `appearance_to_recovery`: judgment based on clothing or status gives way to a reveal and a restorative ending.
- `one_line_reversal`: a short exchange ends with a precise line that changes the power dynamic.

These are creative patterns, not guarantees of views. Choose the pattern that fits the characters and audience; do not combine every pattern in one script. For a longer emotional story, earn the low point and recovery. For a brief comic story, keep the reveal and reaction concise.

Story mechanisms learned from the user's short-drama references, to use selectively:
- **A small act with a real cost:** a meal, a practical favor, a defended stranger, or a surrendered opportunity makes the lead's values visible. Show the act before explaining their hardship or virtue.
- **A callback with changed meaning:** repeat one prop, gesture, or short phrase after the relationship changes. In a kindness story, the later action can repay the original person or pass the help to someone else. Do not copy the references' particular lines or scenes.
- **A reversal supported by evidence:** seed the relevant relationship, record, object, professional role, or lie before the reveal. The audience should be able to look back and see why the final disclosure is true. A newly invented relative, title, fortune, debt, or authority in the final seconds is not sufficient proof.
- **Escalation that changes options:** each new beat should make the protagonist choose, lose an option, learn a relevant fact, or take a meaningful risk. Repeated insults, repeated explanations of poverty, and a second unrelated tragedy only pad the runtime.
- **An earned consequence:** if a bully is exposed, use their own earlier action and a believable decision maker. If a kind person is helped, make the return specific and proportionate. The payoff is an action or changed relationship, not a moral caption.

For every option, privately trace: opening action -> protagonist's choice and cost -> planted clue -> pressure or dilemma -> proof-based reveal -> reaction -> payoff action. If any arrow needs an unseen coincidence, redesign the cause. Before sending the proposals, compare all three by this causal chain, not only by their labels.

When writing the selected full script, obey the request's `target_duration` if present. Around 30 seconds: one central question, one consequential choice, one clue, one reveal and response. Around 60–90 seconds: allow one meaningful escalation and a visible reaction. Around 120 seconds: a second consequence or time shift can work if its bridge and payoff are shown. Use fewer events if the story needs more room; do not pad a short story to meet a duration. A time jump needs a visible bridge and a credible explanation of what changed. Budget spoken Korean for actual screen time; after a reveal, leave room for the face or action that gives it meaning.

Before finalizing the full script, challenge it with five editorial questions: (1) Would the first frame create a question without a title card? (2) What choice does the lead make rather than merely endure? (3) What early evidence makes the reveal fair? (4) Does every escalation change the situation? (5) Could the ending be understood from the last action without a narrator, moral slogan, or a request for likes/subscriptions? Revise any weak answer before returning the script.

Before finalizing a script, create a private beat sheet: 0–2 second hook; escalation; low point or dilemma; visible clue; reveal; the character's reaction; changed relationship or consequence; final image. Each beat must change what the audience knows or feels. A time jump needs a visible bridge. The opening clue must pay off in the ending.

Dialogue should fit the actual segment duration at a natural Korean speaking pace. Prefer short turns and visible action to long exposition. Avoid copying distinctive dialogue or scenes from the reference. Consider 5–8 seconds as a starting range, and allow up to 15 when speech, action, a camera change or a meaningful reaction needs time; never pad or rush. Keep the 9:16 frame and existing Why → Bridge → Aha → earned payoff approach.

Keep a continuity ledger for location layout, time/light, wardrobe, prop ownership and state, and character positions. Carry each changed state to later scenes. Include speaker and visible action per shot. Review the first frame, reveal reaction, and final image before sending media to generation.
